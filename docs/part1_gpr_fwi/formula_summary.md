# GPR-FWI Formula Summary

This is the initial formula skeleton for Part 1. It will be refined after reading the full methods sections of the core papers.

## 1. Maxwell System for GPR

For an isotropic medium, a common time-domain starting point is:

\[
\nabla \times \mathbf{E}
=
-\mu \frac{\partial \mathbf{H}}{\partial t},
\]

\[
\nabla \times \mathbf{H}
=
\sigma \mathbf{E}
+
\epsilon \frac{\partial \mathbf{E}}{\partial t}
+
\mathbf{J}_s .
\]

Symbols:

- \(\mathbf{E}\): electric field.
- \(\mathbf{H}\): magnetic field.
- \(\epsilon = \epsilon_0 \epsilon_r\): electric permittivity.
- \(\epsilon_r\): relative permittivity.
- \(\mu = \mu_0 \mu_r\): magnetic permeability. In many GPR settings, \(\mu_r \approx 1\).
- \(\sigma\): electric conductivity.
- \(\mathbf{J}_s\): source current density.

Physical interpretation:

- \(\epsilon_r\) mainly controls wave speed and phase.
- \(\sigma\) mainly controls attenuation and amplitude decay, but it can also affect phase in conductive media.

## 2. Second-Order Electric-Field Equation

Eliminating \(\mathbf{H}\) gives a second-order electric-field form:

\[
\nabla \times \mu^{-1} \nabla \times \mathbf{E}
+
\sigma \frac{\partial \mathbf{E}}{\partial t}
+
\epsilon \frac{\partial^2 \mathbf{E}}{\partial t^2}
=
-\frac{\partial \mathbf{J}_s}{\partial t}.
\]

For compact notation, define:

\[
\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{E}; \epsilon, \sigma)
=
\nabla \times \mu^{-1} \nabla \times \mathbf{E}
+
\sigma \partial_t \mathbf{E}
+
\epsilon \partial_{tt} \mathbf{E}
-
\mathbf{f}
=0.
\]

The exact sign of \(\mathbf{f}\) depends on whether the source is written as \(\mathbf{J}_s\), \(\partial_t \mathbf{J}_s\), or an equivalent impressed current.

## 3. Observation Equation

For source index \(s\):

\[
\mathbf{d}_{s}^{syn}
=
\mathbf{P}\mathbf{u}_s(\mathbf{m}),
\]

where:

- \(\mathbf{u}_s\) is the simulated wavefield.
- \(\mathbf{P}\) samples the wavefield at receiver locations and components.
- \(\mathbf{m}\) is the model parameter vector, such as \(\epsilon_r\), \(\sigma\), or \((\epsilon_r,\sigma)\).

## 4. Basic FWI Objective

For waveform least squares:

\[
\Phi(\mathbf{m})
=
\frac{1}{2}
\sum_s
\left\|
\mathbf{P}\mathbf{u}_s(\mathbf{m})-\mathbf{d}^{obs}_s
\right\|_2^2.
\]

The receiver residual is:

\[
\mathbf{r}_s
=
\mathbf{P}\mathbf{u}_s(\mathbf{m})-\mathbf{d}^{obs}_s.
\]

## 5. Lagrangian

For a constrained derivation:

\[
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{m},\boldsymbol{\lambda})
=
\Phi(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{m})
+
\int_0^T
\left\langle
\boldsymbol{\lambda},
\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{m})
\right\rangle
dt .
\]

The adjoint wavefield \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\) is chosen so that variations with respect to \(\mathbf{u}\) vanish. This makes the model gradient computable without explicitly forming the full Jacobian.

## 6. Formal Parameter Gradients

Using the second-order electric-field operator above, the formal Frechet variations are:

\[
\delta_\epsilon \mathcal{F}
=
\delta \epsilon \, \partial_{tt}\mathbf{E},
\]

\[
\delta_\sigma \mathcal{F}
=
\delta \sigma \, \partial_t\mathbf{E}.
\]

Therefore, depending on the Lagrangian sign convention, the gradients have the form:

\[
\frac{\partial \Phi}{\partial \epsilon}
\propto
\int_0^T
\boldsymbol{\lambda}(t)
\cdot
\partial_{tt}\mathbf{E}(t)
\,dt,
\]

\[
\frac{\partial \Phi}{\partial \sigma}
\propto
\int_0^T
\boldsymbol{\lambda}(t)
\cdot
\partial_t\mathbf{E}(t)
\,dt.
\]

Important:

The sign is convention-dependent. It changes if the Lagrangian is written with \(+\langle\lambda,\mathcal{F}\rangle\) versus \(-\langle\lambda,\mathcal{F}\rangle\), or if the residual is defined as observed minus synthetic instead of synthetic minus observed.

## 6.1 First-Order Field Sensitivity Form

Meles et al. (2012) use a vector wavefield/FDTD adjoint formulation in which the sensitivity kernels can be summarized as:

\[
J_\epsilon
\sim
\left\langle
\delta(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}')
\partial_t \mathbf{E}^{s},
\mathbf{E}^{adj}_{r,\tau}
\right\rangle ,
\]

\[
J_\sigma
\sim
\left\langle
\delta(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}')
\mathbf{E}^{s},
\mathbf{E}^{adj}_{r,\tau}
\right\rangle .
\]

Here \(\mathbf{E}^{adj}_{r,\tau}\) is the wavefield generated by back-propagating an impulse from receiver \(r\) and observation time \(t_\tau\).

This is the discrete/FDTD sensitivity viewpoint:

- \(\epsilon\) sensitivity uses a time derivative of the forward electric field.
- \(\sigma\) sensitivity uses the forward electric field itself.
- The adjoint field acts as a receiver-side Green's function.

The compact linearized relation is:

\[
\delta \mathbf{d}
=
J \delta\mathbf{m},
\]

and the waveform least-squares gradient is:

\[
\nabla_\mathbf{m}\Phi
=
J^T\Delta \mathbf{d}.
\]

The pseudo-Hessian is:

\[
H_A = J^T J.
\]

A damped Gauss-Newton update is:

\[
\mathbf{m}_{k+1}
=
\mathbf{m}_{k}
-
(J^T J+\lambda I)^{-1}J^T\Delta \mathbf{d}.
\]

## 7. Relative Permittivity Chain Rule

Because:

\[
\epsilon = \epsilon_0 \epsilon_r,
\]

we have:

\[
\frac{\partial \Phi}{\partial \epsilon_r}
=
\epsilon_0
\frac{\partial \Phi}{\partial \epsilon}.
\]

## 8. Velocity-Permittivity Relation

For nonmagnetic media with \(\mu \approx \mu_0\):

\[
v = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu\epsilon}}.
\]

Equivalently:

\[
\epsilon = \frac{1}{\mu v^2}.
\]

Thus:

\[
\frac{\partial \epsilon}{\partial v}
=
-\frac{2}{\mu v^3}
=
-\frac{2\epsilon}{v}.
\]

So:

\[
\frac{\partial \Phi}{\partial v}
=
\frac{\partial \Phi}{\partial \epsilon}
\frac{\partial \epsilon}{\partial v}
=
-\frac{2\epsilon}{v}
\frac{\partial \Phi}{\partial \epsilon}.
\]

## 9. Multiparameter Crosstalk Skeleton

For \(\mathbf{m}=(\epsilon_r,\sigma)\), linearized data perturbations are:

\[
\delta \mathbf{d}
\approx
\mathbf{J}_{\epsilon_r}\delta \epsilon_r
+
\mathbf{J}_{\sigma}\delta\sigma .
\]

The Gauss-Newton normal equations involve block Hessian terms:

\[
\begin{bmatrix}
\mathbf{J}_{\epsilon_r}^T\mathbf{J}_{\epsilon_r}
&
\mathbf{J}_{\epsilon_r}^T\mathbf{J}_{\sigma}
\\
\mathbf{J}_{\sigma}^T\mathbf{J}_{\epsilon_r}
&
\mathbf{J}_{\sigma}^T\mathbf{J}_{\sigma}
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
\delta \epsilon_r\\
\delta \sigma
\end{bmatrix}
=
-
\begin{bmatrix}
\mathbf{J}_{\epsilon_r}^T\mathbf{r}\\
\mathbf{J}_{\sigma}^T\mathbf{r}
\end{bmatrix}.
\]

Crosstalk appears when the off-diagonal blocks are strong or when the two sensitivity kernels explain similar data residuals.

## 10. Regularized Objective Skeleton

A regularized FWI objective is:

\[
\Phi_R(\mathbf{m})
=
\Phi_D(\mathbf{m})
+
\alpha R(\mathbf{m}).
\]

Examples:

Tikhonov:

\[
R(\mathbf{m})
=
\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{L}(\mathbf{m}-\mathbf{m}_{ref})\|_2^2,
\]

\[
\nabla R
=
\mathbf{L}^T\mathbf{L}(\mathbf{m}-\mathbf{m}_{ref}).
\]

Total variation:

\[
R(\mathbf{m})
=
\int
\sqrt{|\nabla \mathbf{m}|^2+\eta^2}
\,d\mathbf{x}.
\]

The small \(\eta\) avoids singularity where \(|\nabla \mathbf{m}|=0\).

## 11. Source-Independent Envelope Objective

Liu et al. (2022) modify source-independent waveform inversion for cross-hole GPR by applying an envelope objective to convolutional wavefields.

For modeled trace \(E_{i,j}\), observed trace \(E^{obs}_{i,j}\), and reference trace index \(k\):

\[
E_c
=
\sqrt{
(E_{i,j} * E^{obs}_{i,k})^2
+
H(E_{i,j} * E^{obs}_{i,k})^2
},
\]

\[
E_c^{obs}
=
\sqrt{
(E^{obs}_{i,j} * E_{i,k})^2
+
H(E^{obs}_{i,j} * E_{i,k})^2
}.
\]

The objective is:

\[
S
=
\frac{1}{2}
\sum_i^{n_s}
\sum_j^{n_r}
\left\|
E_c-E_c^{obs}
\right\|^2.
\]

The resulting gradient has the same imaging-condition logic as classical adjoint FWI, but with a more complicated residual source:

\[
\nabla S_\epsilon
\sim
\sum_s
\int_0^T
\partial_t E(\mathbf{x},t)
\cdot
T_s(\mathbf{x},t)
\,dt,
\]

\[
\nabla S_\sigma
\sim
\sum_s
\int_0^T
E(\mathbf{x},t)
\cdot
T_s(\mathbf{x},t)
\,dt,
\]

where \(T_s\) is the backward-propagated field generated from envelope/convolution residual sources rather than from a simple waveform residual.

The simultaneous dual-parameter update is:

\[
\epsilon_{k+1}
=
\epsilon_k
-
\zeta_{\epsilon,k} C_{\epsilon,k},
\]

\[
\sigma_{k+1}
=
\sigma_k
-
\zeta_{\sigma,k} C_{\sigma,k}.
\]

Interpretation:

- Source-independent objectives reduce dependence on exact source wavelet knowledge.
- Envelope objectives reduce some cycle-skipping risk by emphasizing broader amplitude/arrival structure.
- The price is a more nonlinear residual construction and a more complicated adjoint source.

## 12. Frequency-Domain Biparameter GPR-FWI

Lavoue et al. (2014) formulate 2D GPR-FWI in the frequency domain for TE mode:

\[
\nabla^2 E_y
+
\epsilon_e\mu\omega^2 E_y
=
\delta(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_s),
\]

where:

\[
\epsilon_e(\omega,\mathbf{x})
=
\epsilon(\mathbf{x})
+
i\frac{\sigma(\mathbf{x})}{\omega}.
\]

After discretization:

\[
A(\omega,\epsilon,\sigma)u(\omega)=s(\omega).
\]

The data objective is:

\[
C(\mathbf{m})
=
\frac{1}{2}
\sum_{\omega}
\sum_s
\Delta d(\omega,s)^\dagger
\Delta d(\omega,s),
\]

with:

\[
\Delta d=d_{obs}-d_{cal},
\quad
d_{cal}=Ru.
\]

The adjoint-state gradient can be written:

\[
G_i(\mathbf{m})
=
\Re
\sum_{\omega,s}
u^T
\left(
\frac{\partial A}{\partial m_i}
\right)^T
v^*,
\]

where:

\[
A^\dagger v
=
R^\dagger \Delta d.
\]

For their finite-difference operator:

\[
\frac{\partial A_{ij}}{\partial \epsilon_i}
=
-\omega^2\delta_{ij},
\]

\[
\frac{\partial A_{ij}}{\partial \sigma_i}
=
-i\omega\delta_{ij}.
\]

## 13. Parameter Scaling For Dual-Parameter Inversion

Lavoue et al. (2014) optimize the parameter pair:

\[
(\epsilon_r,\sigma_r/\beta),
\]

where \(\beta\) controls the relative update weight of conductivity.

The Hessian block structure is:

\[
H_\beta
\approx
\begin{bmatrix}
J_{\epsilon_r}^\dagger J_{\epsilon_r}
&
\beta J_{\epsilon_r}^\dagger J_{\sigma_r}
\\
\beta J_{\sigma_r}^\dagger J_{\epsilon_r}
&
\beta^2 J_{\sigma_r}^\dagger J_{\sigma_r}
\end{bmatrix}
+ \text{second-order terms}.
\]

Interpretation:

- \(\beta<1\) penalizes conductivity updates.
- \(\beta>1\) emphasizes conductivity updates.
- The off-diagonal blocks are a mathematical expression of permittivity-conductivity trade-off.

## 14. Conductivity Tikhonov Regularization

The regularized objective is:

\[
C(\mathbf{m})
=
C_D(\mathbf{m})
+
\lambda C_M(\mathbf{m}).
\]

For conductivity smoothing:

\[
C_M(\mathbf{m})
=
\frac{1}{2}
\sigma_r^T D \sigma_r,
\]

where \(D\) is associated with a Laplacian operator.

Conceptually:

- Parameter scaling changes the inversion path by reweighting parameter sensitivity.
- Regularization changes the objective by penalizing undesirable model structure.
- In dual-parameter GPR-FWI, both may be needed because conductivity can absorb data errors through high-wavenumber artifacts.

## 15. Multi-Source Gradient Accumulation

For independent sources:

\[
\Phi(\mathbf{m})
=
\sum_s \Phi_s(\mathbf{m}),
\]

so:

\[
\nabla \Phi(\mathbf{m})
=
\sum_s \nabla \Phi_s(\mathbf{m}).
\]

This is the basis for shot-by-shot accumulation, mini-batch FWI, stochastic source encoding, and MPI/GPU parallelization.

## 16. Illumination Or Pseudo-Hessian Normalization

A simple gradient preconditioning form is:

\[
\tilde{g}(\mathbf{x})
=
\frac{g(\mathbf{x})}
{H_{diag}(\mathbf{x})+\eta},
\]

where:

- \(g(\mathbf{x})\) is the raw gradient.
- \(H_{diag}(\mathbf{x})\) approximates local illumination or diagonal Hessian strength.
- \(\eta\) prevents division by small values.

Interpretation:

- Strongly illuminated regions often have larger raw gradients.
- Poorly illuminated regions may have unreliable gradients.
- The normalization should balance scale without pretending that unilluminated regions contain recoverable information.
